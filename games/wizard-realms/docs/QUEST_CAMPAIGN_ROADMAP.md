# Wizard Realms quest campaign roadmap

## What a quest should feel like

A quest is a reason to use the world, not a checklist placed over it. The player should see a problem, infer a plausible action, try the game's normal tools, and get a visible, saved payoff. Main-story quests always have deterministic completion rewards. Optional discoveries can be surprising, but randomness must not block the only way onward. A journal tracks the current chapter and completed history without replacing world clues, map landmarks, or interaction prompts.

Use a varied rhythm: discovery, construction, magic, excavation, trade, creature care, then combat. Give the player something immediately useful every few chapters. Name the exact skill XP and character XP separately. A coin payout should be meaningful relative to nearby shops, not an arbitrary number that trivializes later mount prices.

The fun test for a chapter is a small story with a world-visible problem, a clue, a choice of useful action, a complication or discovery, and a payoff that changes what the player can do next. Avoid chains that only ask the player to collect an arbitrary count and return. A new route should reveal a place worth visiting; a spell should solve a problem that ordinary tools cannot; a reward should become visible or useful within the next few minutes. Revisit earlier places after the player's new ability changes their meaning.

After that, prototype one optional discovery quest and one first-combat quest as different rhythms. An optional highland signal could reward finding a vantage point and using a learned spell with coin and Spellcraft XP, plus a persistent map clue. A first-combat contract should teach readable attacks and recovery against a single encounter, guarantee a visibly equipped armor piece with tested protection, and make a rare cosmetic or crafting drop an exciting bonus rather than the only route forward. These are proposals, not implemented features or promises of existing combat. Quest turn-ins need a character or place the player can recognize and return to, so rewards feel earned in the world rather than appearing from a menu.

## Rewards and drop rules

1. A saved quest claim derives its reward from authoritative state under a checked witness. Client UI can preview, never choose or mint currency. A duplicate or failed claim must change nothing. Historical versions remain replayable.
2. Starter rewards favor tools, routes, capacity and practical clothing; later rewards can offer a gear choice when both choices have distinct tested effects. A visually different garment is not called armor until damage mitigation exists.
3. First true armor milestone defines health/damage, defense or resistance, durability/repair, level/skill requirements, and visible equipment together. Armor quality tiers can then be compared meaningfully. Shop, quest and drop gear should have understandable tradeoffs, not one permanently dominant item.
4. A combat drop belongs to an authoritative defeated encounter, with unique claim ownership, a documented probability or deterministic pity/guarantee policy, overflow handling, save/reload and rollback tests. Main-story progress always has a nonrandom route. Before combat exists, call finds “cache rewards” or “materials,” not enemy drops.
5. Reward duplicates get a fair alternative such as coins or a material. The alternative should reflect actual shop value. Inventory-full and escrow cases must be explicit, not silent item loss. Sold quest finds must not soft-lock completion when a persistent world achievement already proves the act.

## Quest UX and proof

Early guidance should be contextual and fade as the player demonstrates mastery. When a player reaches a blocked cliff, prompt the ladder recipe and marked site; at a river, point to a bridge or later boat. A hidden cache should need a clue and spell, not invisible coordinates. The journal says what happened and what is next, but the world itself remains legible.

For each chapter prove: discovery, normal-control completion, incorrect/remote/repeat action refusal, save/reload, usable reward, and mobile/desktop non-occlusion. A browser smoke with a constructed fixture can prove a segment, but cannot be called a full fresh-player journey. Periodically repeat a fresh-start blind playtest to detect confusing dead ends and clunky controls. The full-game goal remains open until the connected world, campaign, economy, gear, combat, mounts and endgame are genuinely playable together.
